R21 Mamet, memo to the writers of The Unit, 19 Oct 2005 Every scene must answer three questions: who wants what, what happens if they don't get it, why now. The answers are "litmus paper." P (memo reprinted in full, slashfilm.com; 2amtheatre.com)

2. Gap analysis: the current three scenes against R21 to R24

Finding: two scenes fail all three of Mamet's questions, and Nicky passes two. In all three the secret is a trait (a fear, a soft spot, a fact) rather than a choice under pressure, so the three cards repeat one state three times, which R24 calls a dead beat. Nobody on stage would be changed by learning the secret, so the house has nothing to fear on anyone's behalf. Hitchcock's bomb (R6) is in the player's hand, but nobody is sitting on it.

Three engine-level gaps follow from that, and the scripts below answer each: - No scene before the staging. The player reads one line of setup and stages. There is no intention and obstacle on stage before the cards arrive. Fix: an AT RISE page of eight to twelve lines of real script (D2). - No ending. The verdict closes the scene; the play never turns. Fix: a CURTAIN beat, two to four lines, that reframes the secret after the verdict (D3). This is R22's failure that propels, and R5's recognition arising from the incidents. - Cards as parallel samples, not beats. OBJECT, MOVE and LINE should be beat 1, 2 and 3 of one escalating scene, so each card raises the pressure on the last (R24). The engine already orders them; the writing must use the order.

4. The seats

SEAT: Mamet

Every kept scene answers the three questions in the at-rise lines, before the player touches a card, which is where the old scenes left a hole. My test for every SHOW card is R2's silent movie: if the card needs its words to work, it belongs in LINE or it fails. OBJECT and MOVE cards in section 5 are written to read with the sound off. The protagonist must fail at something at the curtain, or win at a cost, because the failure is what makes the house walk out arguing (R22). Watch item: the TELL cards are too easy to spot when they are cartoons. A TELL card should be the line a tired first-draft writer would actually write ("As you know, Diane"), so the player has to recognize the habit in themselves. Nobody learns anything from rejecting a banner. SIGNED Mamet seat (lens: R2, R3, R21, R22), 2026-11-05

SEAT: Sorkin

Intention and obstacle sit in the first two lines of every at-rise, and the obstacle is a person in the room with a want of their own, never weather or a feeling. I pressed each one (R23). OFFICE HOURS holds: a scholarship against a reputation. TRANSLATION holds hardest, because the obstacle is love; the daughter is lying to protect the person she is lying to. THE POOL nearly broke under pressing until Bev got her own secret at the curtain. The cards are tactics, and tactics are character, so each SHOW card answers "what does this person do to keep it?" and not "what does this person feel?" Dialogue should have rhythm: short lines that turn on a repeated word ("I need a C. I'm not asking for a C."). The audience should be a half-step ahead of the people on stage, never a full step behind. SIGNED Sorkin seat (lens: R23), 2026-11-05

SEAT: Saunders

Each scene must escalate (R24). I read the three cards as three pulses (R25) and asked what each one changes. OBJECT puts the secret in the room, MOVE puts it in danger, and LINE puts it in someone's mouth. If a card only repeats the last pulse, cut it. The curtain must change what the whole scene meant, not just end it: the banker spoke Polish the whole time, the inspector saw the boxes, Bev played the numbers on her own. Pattern and variation: the curtain should rhyme with the at-rise. Office Hours opens on "I brought the syllabus" and closes on "Give me a blue book." A second watch item belongs to me. The scenes must stay kind to everyone in them. No one on stage is a villain, and each has a want the player can respect, so the player directs people instead of judging them. SIGNED Saunders seat (lens: R24, R25), 2026-11-05

SEAT: Coordinator

Horvath's test is whether the task separates seeing from doing, and the beat structure makes it cleaner than before. The player now judges a card against the scene's pressure, not against a trait, which is a harder and truer discrimination. The at-rise page must be short enough to hold in working memory. Twelve lines is the ceiling, and the secret must sit on its own line above the cards so the player never re-reads the script to find it. The curtain gives a consolidation beat, so it must hold on screen until the player moves on and must never auto-advance. SIGNED Coordinator (Horvath), 2026-11-05

SEAT: Stranger

Cold read on a phone: the at-rise is a script, and a script is a wall unless it plays. Each line should appear as its own block with the speaker in small caps above it, and the scene should open on the set with the first two lines visible without a scroll. Players should tap to reveal the rest, so the page never scrolls itself (Brief First ruling, 2026-10-14). Speaker names stay in text and are never carried by colour. The curtain needs a visible end mark (CURTAIN) so a screen reader hears the scene close. SIGNED Stranger, 2026-11-05

5. The five scenes

Format for each: the three questions, the secret, the escalation ladder, the AT RISE script, nine cards (beat 1 OBJECT, beat 2 MOVE, beat 3 LINE; SHOW, TELL, INVISIBLE in each), and the CURTAIN. Device codes refer to the gap analysis (S, T, I) and the research log (R).

SCENE A — OFFICE HOURS

  • Who wants what: Marcus wants to see his final exam. Professor Ruth Adler wants him out of her office before he asks again.
  • If not: A D costs Marcus his scholarship. A grade she cannot produce costs Ruth her name in front of the appeals committee.
  • Why now: Grades lock at noon. It is 11:40.
  • THE SECRET: Ruth lost Marcus's final exam. She gave him a D from memory.
  • Ladder: beat 1 puts the missing exam in the room; beat 2 puts Ruth in a search she has to hide; beat 3 makes her talk about an exam she never read.

AT RISE. INT. FACULTY OFFICE, LAST DAY OF FINALS, 11:40 A.M. Stacks of blue books. A wall clock.

MARCUS The portal says D.

RUTH The portal is correct.

MARCUS I need a C. I'm not asking for a C. I'm asking to see the exam.

RUTH Exams stay with the department.

MARCUS The syllabus says I can see it. Page four.

(He holds up the syllabus. A sticky note marks page four.)

RUTH You brought the syllabus.

MARCUS I brought the syllabus.

RUTH Sit down, Marcus.

(He sits. She stays standing.)

Beat Kind Card Device
OBJECT SHOW The blue books stand in one tall stack with alphabet tabs. The B tab sits flat on the A tab with nothing between them. Ruth squares the stack and keeps her palm on top of it. S3 set as evidence
OBJECT TELL A sticky note on Ruth's monitor, big enough for the back row, reads FIND BELL EXAM!!! T1 placard
OBJECT INVISIBLE Ruth remembers the train, the window seat, and the tote bag on the rack above her. I1 memory, no trigger
MOVE SHOW While she tells him the exam is filed downstairs, Ruth opens her own desk drawers one at a time, top to bottom, and shuts each one harder than the last. S5 failed task; words against action (R3)
MOVE TELL Ruth turns to the house, lifts both empty hands and shrugs. T4 charade
MOVE INVISIBLE Ruth feels her face go hot. I2 labeled feeling
LINE SHOW RUTH: Your last answer had a strong ending. / MARCUS: I didn't finish the last answer. I ran out of time. The reaction (gap B; R3, R7)
LINE TELL RUTH: As you know, Marcus, exams sometimes get misplaced at the end of term. T7 as you know
LINE INVISIBLE RUTH (thinking): The train. It's on the train. I5 spoken thought (R15)

CURTAIN.

(The clock reads 11:58. Ruth sits for the first time.)

RUTH I lost it.

MARCUS Then give me a blue book. I've got eighteen minutes.

(She hands him one. CURTAIN.)

SCENE B — TRANSLATION

  • Who wants what: Dorota wants the loan to buy the bakery she has run for someone else for twenty years. Her daughter Lina, sixteen, wants her mother not to hear "no" in front of this man. Mr. Pratt, the loan officer, wants to close the file.
  • If not: Dorota signs a lease tomorrow on money that is not coming.
  • Why now: The landlord wants her signature at nine a.m.
  • THE SECRET: The bank said no. Lina is telling her mother it said yes.
  • Ladder: beat 1 takes the evidence off the desk; beat 2 gets her mother out of the room; beat 3 forces Lina to translate a question she cannot answer.

AT RISE. INT. BANK, A LOAN OFFICER'S DESK, 4:20 P.M. A box from the bakery, tied with string, sits on the desk. Dorota brought it for Mr. Pratt.

PRATT I'm sorry. The answer is no.

(Lina turns to her mother.)

LINA (in Polish) He says they're very impressed.

DOROTA (in Polish) Ask him when.

LINA (to Pratt) She wants to know why.

PRATT No credit history. Twenty years, all cash.

LINA (in Polish) He says twenty years of cash is very rare.

DOROTA (in Polish) Tell him I paid cash for flour, I paid cash for rent, and I will pay him cash.

LINA (to Pratt) She says thank you for your time.
