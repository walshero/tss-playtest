The course in one look

What you sign up for

You analyze and experiment in creative nonfiction, poetry, drama and fiction. You read published writers and your classmates, you practice with short exercises, and you share drafts in workshop. At the end you revise your best work into a portfolio and write an artist statement about what you learned and what you value as a writer.

How each unit runs

Every genre turns the same six ways

The schedule tags each task with one of six verbs. Every genre runs through all six. Then the next genre starts the turn again, one level up, and you carry the earlier moves with you.

  • ReadSellers, published writers, craft guides
  • Watchshort talks by working writers
  • Respondpractice exercises and drafts
  • Discussa primary post, then replies
  • Writea new piece in the genre
  • Workshopshare one piece, read your group

Tight Spiral Studios borrows its name from this loop: try it, get feedback, take on a little more, and go again.

Four genres

The four workshops

Each workshop asks for two short pieces or one longer one. You revise one piece, post it, and read the work of your group. Word counts and weeks below come from the Fall 2026 online schedule.

Workshop 1 · Weeks 2–3

Creative Nonfiction

You write
Two true pieces from Sellers exercises, Secret World and Moving Images, 750–1500 words each. You revise one for workshop.
You study
Sellers chapters 1 and 2, “What Is Creative Nonfiction?”, a Peter Elbow film trailer, and a TED talk on giving feedback.
The table
Small groups. You leave comments in the margins and an end-of-document response.

Workshop 2 · Weeks 4–6

Poetry

You write
A Where I’m From poem after George Ella Lyon and a free verse poem with no rhyme scheme, 150 words or more each. You pick one for workshop.
You study
Sellers chapters 3 through 5 on components, images and energy, “Reading a Poem: 20 Strategies,” talks by Billy Collins, James Geary and Robert Pinsky, and a packet of free verse poems.
The table
Small groups named Earth, Wind, Water and Fire.

Workshop 3 · Weeks 6–10

The 10-Minute Play

You write
A complete stage play of 1000–2000 words, formatted the way Sellers shows in chapter 10. Hold a table reading so it lands between nine and eleven minutes.
You study
Sellers chapter 6 on tension, three 10-minute plays (The Sculptor’s Funeral, Going Viral, Family 2.0), the Master Plot Checklist, and how to avoid on-the-nose dialogue.
The table
Six readers take each play, two through each of three lenses: the audience’s experience, tension and stakes, and dialogue and stage directions.

If the audience doesn’t see it or hear it, they won’t know it. Write for a stage, not a screen: no car chases, no rapid scene changes, no “to be continued.”

Workshop 4 · Weeks 10–12

Flash Fiction

You write
An original story of 750–2000 words in any fictional genre. It has to be new: no fan fiction, no true events, no chapter one.
You study
Kurt Vonnegut on the shapes of stories, Louise Tondeur’s guide to flash fiction, Jamaica Kincaid’s “Girl,” Gabriel García Márquez’s “One of These Days,” Michelle Ross’s “Palate Cleanser,” and Heidi Pitlor on what makes a good story.
The table
The whole class comments, so every writer who submits gets real attention.

In the Spring 2026 in-person section, Workshop 4 was a short story of 1000–4000 words, built on “The Lesson,” “Blood,” and George Saunders on story.

The heart of the course

How the workshop table works

Workshop comments count as much as your own writing. No one needs prior workshop experience. You read, watch and discuss how to give useful feedback before the first round.

  1. Share it so anyone with the link can comment.Write your name and a real title at the top, and add your professor as Editor.
  2. Freeze the draft.Once you post, don’t revise that document, or the group ends up reading different versions.
  3. Writers lurk.You may leave questions for readers before the deadline. After that, you read the comments and wait until commenting closes.
  4. Comment in the margins, then sum up.Select a word or two, not a paragraph, and finish with an end-of-document response.
  5. Suggest, don’t edit.The writer owns the writing. Name a pattern once instead of correcting every line.
  6. Be specific.Say why a moment works. “Great story” is fine, and it helps more with a reason attached.

    Grading

    A grade built for risk-taking

    The grading policy, adapted from writing teacher Peter Elbow, lets you concentrate on your writing instead of your grade. You are guaranteed a final B when you do four things:

    1. Engage with the class consistently.
    2. Take an active part in the discussion board and comment thoughtfully in workshops.
    3. Turn in work on time. A workshop course depends on it.
    4. Meet each workshop piece’s specifications for genre, focus and length, shared with the right settings.

    What turns a B into an A

    An A means the effort shows on the page. The syllabus names ten criteria.

    DEADLINEOn time, shared correctly.
    GUIDELINESFits the assignment.
    THEMECarries meaning and insight that holds a reader.
    CONVENTIONSUses the genre’s structures: stanzas, dialogue, paragraphing, point of view.
    CREATIVITYUses concrete detail and finds new ways of seeing.
    DEVELOPMENTTakes the time on the page it needs to stand alone.
    CLARITYKeeps the reader engaged without getting lost.
    LANGUAGEUses concrete images, varied sentences, and no word packages.
    CRAFTApplies strategies from the course.
    REVISIONReshapes, extends or deepens the work, then proofreads it.

    When life happens

    If an emergency hits, email before the deadline, or as soon as you can, to ask for an extension. An extension for real circumstances doesn’t affect your grade. Late discussion posts earn up to 70–80 percent credit. If you finish workshop comments late, send an email so the work gets credit.

    Academic integrity and AI

    A human-only class

    “Let the machines talk to each other and let’s keep this a human only class.”From the Fall 2026 syllabus

    Submitting AI-generated content, or running your work through generative editing software, is not allowed. The syllabus compares it to sending a robot to the gym to lift your weights: you only get stronger by doing the work. When a question about authenticity comes up, your professor usually checks a draft’s version history.

    The usual rules apply too. Name the source any time you borrow words, ideas, statistics or facts, and don’t turn in someone else’s work or the same work for two courses. If you’re unsure, ask before you submit.

    Content and care

    Room for hard subjects, safety for readers

    You have real freedom in what you write, and difficult subjects like loss, injustice and discrimination belong in this room. The line falls between writing that explores harm and writing that celebrates it, the same line many literary magazines draw in their submission guidelines.

    As a reader, you may skip material that feels too difficult. You can tell your professor privately about any content you’d rather avoid, without giving a reason, and writers can add a short content warning at the top of a piece.

    The finish line

    Portfolio and artist statement

    There is no final exam. You close the term by revising your strongest work into a portfolio and writing an artist statement about your influences, your values as a writer, and what you accomplished.

    • Bonus workshop. If you missed a workshop or want eyes on a portfolio piece, you can submit one more time. It’s optional if you’re caught up.
    • Course reflection. The last discussion board asks what worked and what didn’t. The course gets redesigned from these posts.
    • Feedback by email. About a week after the portfolio is due, you get comments, a portfolio grade and your final grade.

    In the in-person section the term ends out loud: the class attends the lumière literary magazine launch and closes with five-minute readings from the semester’s work.

    The studio builds short games that line up with the schedule. They’re practice, not homework, and none of them is graded.

    Poetry · Weeks 4–5

    Kireji Pond

    Catch the fresh image, smash the cliché, let the rest go, and build a three-line poem that turns.

    Images · Week 5, again Week 13

    Reading Lamp

    Trade overused phrases for things a reader can see.

    10-Minute Play · Weeks 7–9

    Dress Rehearsal

    Each scene hides a secret. Pick the move, the line or the silence that stages it, and find out whether the house saw it or was told it.

    Before Workshop 4 · Weeks 9–10

    The Asking Room

    Sit at a workshop table with three drafts, notice what each one is doing, and say it so it helps the writer.

    Flash Fiction · Weeks 10–11, again Week 13

    Sandbags

    Every word is a sandbag. Cut the ones a flash piece can fly without.
